The Data

This report compares two country-level indicators for the year 2020:

  • Consumption-based CO2 per capita — tonnes of CO2 per person, counting emissions embodied in what a country consumes rather than what it produces.
  • GDP per capita — PPP and inflation-adjusted international dollars, from the Penn World Table.

For 2020, 192 countries report CO2 data, 173 report GDP, and 173 carry both — the set used throughout this study.

Why GDP per capita, PPP (Penn World Table)? Three reasons make it the right economic yardstick here:
  1. Per-capita matching. Emissions are measured per person, so wealth must be too — otherwise a country's sheer population size would distort the comparison rather than its standard of living.
  2. Real consumption (PPP). Market exchange rates track currency trading; PPP tracks what people can actually buy locally. Since emissions follow physical activity — heating, transport, manufacturing — PPP is the closer proxy for a nation's true material footprint.
  3. Comparability over time and space (PWT). The Penn World Table is the academic benchmark precisely because it adjusts for both cross-country price differences and inflation, filtering out short-term market noise.

The Relationship

Plotting the 173 countries directly, with GDP on a log scale, the trend is unmistakable — and the interesting stories live in the outliers above and below the line.

What the Picture Says

In 2020, CO2 per capita and GDP per capita are strongly and positively associated (Pearson r = 0.82). Put plainly: the richest fifth of these economies emit roughly 29.0× as much carbon per person as the poorest fifth.

The maps make the overlap visceral — North America, the Gulf, Europe, and parts of East Asia and Oceania run hot on both measures, while much of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia sits pale on both.

But the line is not destiny. Above it sit the carbon-heavy outliers: Gulf oil producers and small wealthy states like Qatar and Luxembourg pair very high income with exceptional emissions. Below it sit economies — Singapore among them — that have decoupled some of their prosperity from carbon. That spread is the policy story: development and energy-intensive consumption move together today, but the distance from the trend line shows how much room national choices still leave open.

Notes on the Build

The original draft rendered its world maps with map_data("world") and coord_map(). That combination drew a faint horizontal “scar” across the globe: polygons crossing the antimeridian (Russia, Fiji, the Aleutian Islands) had a closing edge drawn straight across the map. This version renders geometry with sf + rnaturalearth under a Robinson projection, which handles the wrap correctly and removes the artifact — with a corrected map_data fallback included for environments where sf is unavailable.
