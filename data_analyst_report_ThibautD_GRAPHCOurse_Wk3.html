▸ 01 / Introduction

What the data is, and what we asked of it

This report works from a sample of 400 Data Analyst job postings drawn from the open-source picklesueat/Jobs-Titles-In-Data-Science repository, enriched with extracted fields for minimum years of experience, programming-language requirements, ownership type, and industry sector.

The aim is narrow and honest — to test two intuitions: that experience commands salary, and that knowing Python or R commands a premium. The answers, as the data show, are softer than the intuitions. The interactive controls below allow filtering the same charts by ownership and industry, where the picture sharpens or shifts.

Two patterns persist across most filter combinations: the relationship between years of experience and salary stays weak (the line is nearly flat in nearly every slice), and the median pay across Python / R / Both / Neither stays remarkably close. Where filters do shift the picture — most visibly when narrowing to a single high-paying industry — the small-n caveat (next section) becomes severe.

// Limitations With 10 industry sectors and 5 ownership categories, many filter combinations yield very small subsets (n < 20). Trend lines and medians on tiny samples are unstable — a single posting can move them materially. Treat any narrow slice as a sketch, not a finding. ▸ 03 / Years of Experience

Does seniority set the price?

Across the unfiltered sample, the relationship between minimum experience required and median salary is far weaker than expected. The trend tilts upward — roughly $670 per additional year — but the Pearson correlation is only r = 0.060. Roles asking for two years and roles asking for ten overlap heavily in pay.

Two readings are plausible. The first is that experience is a weak signal in this segment — Data Analyst postings cluster around a similar mid-band regardless of seniority demanded. The second is that other variables (location, industry, company size) carry most of the salary signal and are washing out experience here as a single-variable view. The interactive controls above let you test the second reading directly.

// Limitations The unfiltered scatter uses 309 rows after dropping missing-experience values. Experience was extracted from free-text descriptions, so the field is noisy, and high-experience postings (10+ years) are sparse, which makes the slope unstable.
▸ 04 / Language Requirements

Python, R, both, or neither?

Grouping by which data languages each posting names, the medians land remarkably close: Python $69K, Both $68K, Neither $68K, R $62K. The most striking observation is not that any single language pays more — it is that Neither is by far the largest category (n = 255). Many Data Analyst postings in this set simply do not name R or Python.

// Limitations The R group has only 15 postings — too few to read the lower median as a real penalty. A single outlier in such a small group moves the median materially. Absence of a language in the posting text means "not mentioned," not "not required."
▸ 05 / Personal Reflection

A wayfinder's note

Before the chart, before the line, there was the open water — a sea of postings, no compass, no sextant, only the stars of a column header and the smell of salt on a CSV.
The wayfinders of the Pacific knew this: you do not row each wave alone. You read the swell, you trust the bird, you let the current carry the canoe from one green island to the next.
An API, I learn this week, is that current. A request goes out — a response returns — and what would have taken a season of paddling arrives by the time the tea is poured.
I am, at last, happy to understand it. Happy to use it. Happy to feel the canoe move under hands that finally know which way the wind has been blowing all this time.
A horizon of possibilities opens across my current work — atolls I had only seen on the map, now within reach of a single, well-formed call.
One note, though, before the next voyage: the key to the canoe is not for the deck. Remove the API key before you share the chart. Remove it before you paste into an LLM. A wayfinder keeps the star map close — and does not leave it in the sand.
