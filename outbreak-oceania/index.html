Dengue Shadow

Two weeks after Tropical Cyclone Moana flooded the northern districts of Vanu-Toka, clinics are swamped with a dengue-like febrile illness. You are the lead field epidemiologist. Everything you decide will run off one thing: the data. Ten rooms, real calculations, one moving leaderboard.

How this works. You'll download a line list and work in your own spreadsheet, derive a case definition, clean and analyse the data, survive a laboratory plot twist, run an analytic study, and design the response — including the AI prompt that writes it. The clock runs the whole time. Wrong answers, hints, and Advisor questions add time penalties, so accuracy beats speed.

Room 03 · Data quality

Clean the line list

Your count is only as good as your data. Clean it, and report what cleaning did.

Cleaning steps

  1. Remove duplicates on case_id (Data ▸ Remove Duplicates). Note how many rows were removed.
  2. Standardise village names — fix variants like "taro valley", "Taro Vally", "TARO VALLEY" to Taro Valley.
  3. Drop impossible ages (<1 or >120) and records with a blank onset date.
  4. Report: (a) duplicates removed, (b) % completeness of onset_date after de-duplication, (c) clean suspected-case count.
Completeness = records with a non-blank onset ÷ total rows after de-duplication, as a %. The clean suspected count is your Room 02 logic re-applied after removing duplicates, bad ages, and blank onsets.

Room 05 · The epidemic curve

Read the outbreak's shape

Here is the epi curve built from your cleaned onset dates. Classify the transmission pattern, then back-calculate when people were exposed.
What pattern does this curve show?

Back-calculation

Leptospirosis incubation is typically 5–14 days (range 2–30). The curve rises from ~18 Oct and sustains its peak around 21–25 Oct. Using the typical incubation against the peak, in which window did the main exposure occur?
A single sharp spike = point source (one shared meal). A sustained plateau over many days = continuous exposure to a common environmental source. Successive growing waves = person-to-person. Peak (~23 Oct) minus the typical 5–14 day incubation brackets the cyclone-flood week.
Room 06 · The laboratory

The results change everything

INCOMING · NATIONAL REFERENCE LAB
"We ran confirmatory tests on the suspected cases. Dengue is mostly negative. You need to see the Leptospira results."

Build a 2×2 and interpret

Join the lab file to your clean cases on case_id. Cross-tabulate clinical suspected (yes/no) against lab-confirmed leptospirosis (yes/no), then compute:
  • PPV of the clinical definition for leptospirosis = a ÷ (a+b)
  • Dengue positivity among all tested = dengue-positive ÷ total tested × 100
It's leptospirosis. Now revise the case definition into tiers — pick the correct Confirmed tier:
a = suspected AND lepto-confirmed; b = suspected AND not confirmed. The clinical definition is highly sensitive but not specific — many "dengue-like" patients are really leptospirosis, and dengue tests come back negative. A confirmed case needs laboratory proof (MAT or PCR positive), not just symptoms.
Room 07 · Analytic epidemiology

Cohort analysis

For each exposure (contact_floodwater, barefoot_fieldwork, rodent_contact): build a 2×2 of exposure against illness, compute the attack rate in the exposed vs. the unexposed, and the relative risk. Then identify the exposure with the strongest, biologically plausible association. (Stuck on the formula? The hint has the full method — but it costs time.)
Relative risk (RR) compares the risk of illness in the exposed group with the risk in the unexposed group.
RR = Risk in exposed groupRisk in unexposed group

Room 10 · Containment

Sign off the response

Select the evidence-based intervention package for this outbreak. Choose carefully — a measure aimed at the wrong disease will fail the sign-off.
